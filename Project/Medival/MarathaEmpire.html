Deep Dive: Explore the comprehensive history of the Maratha Empire — a powerful confederacy that challenged Mughal dominance, pioneered guerrilla warfare, and laid the early foundations for modern Indian federalism and naval power.
1674 CE

Foundation & Shivaji Maharaj

The Birth of Swarajya (Self-Rule)

Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj
  • Born in 1630, inspired by his mother Jijabai and guru Samarth Ramdas
  • Started with a small band of Mavala soldiers, capturing the Torna fort in 1646
  • Famously defeated the Bijapur general Afzal Khan at Pratapgad (1659)
  • Crowned as Chhatrapati (sovereign king) at Raigad Fort in 1674
Core Principles
  • Swarajya: The concept of self-rule and independence from the Delhi Sultanate and Mughal Empire
  • Religious tolerance: Protected mosques and employed Muslims in high administrative and military positions (e.g., Siddi Ibrahim, Darya Sarang)
  • Abolished the Jizya tax and prohibited the destruction of places of worship
  • Established a disciplined, decentralized administrative framework
Legacy of Shivaji: He died in 1680, leaving behind a robust, independent kingdom that served as the foundation for a pan-Indian empire. He is revered as the "Father of the Indian Navy" for his coastal fortifications and shipbuilding.
1713-1761 CE

The Era of the Peshwas

Transformation into a Pan-Indian Confederacy

Rise of the Peshwa Office
  • After a period of instability following Shivaji's death, Balaji Vishwanath became Peshwa (Prime Minister) in 1713
  • His son, Baji Rao I (1720-1740), is considered the greatest Maratha military strategist
  • Baji Rao I famously stated: "Let us strike at the trunk of the withering tree (Mughals), and the branches will fall off themselves"
  • Expanded Maratha influence to Delhi, Malwa, Gujarat, and Bundelkhand
The Confederacy System
  • Under Balaji Baji Rao, the empire evolved into a loose confederacy
  • Semi-independent Maratha chiefs governed vast regions: Scindia (Gwalior), Holkar (Indore), Bhonsle (Nagpur), and Gaekwad (Baroda)
  • The Peshwa in Pune acted as the nominal head and coordinator of the confederacy
  • Maratha power reached its zenith, controlling much of the Indian subcontinent
Administrative Shift: While the Chhatrapati remained the symbolic sovereign in Satara, real political and military power shifted to the Peshwa in Pune, transforming the governance structure into a federal-like system.
17th-18th Century

Military Innovation

Guerrilla Warfare and Naval Supremacy

Ganimi Kava (Guerrilla Warfare)
  • Avoided pitched battles against larger, heavily armed Mughal armies
  • Relied on rapid, surprise attacks, ambushes, and swift retreats into the mountains
  • Light cavalry (Bargirs and Shiledars) was the backbone of the army, capable of covering vast distances quickly
  • Masterful use of local terrain and geography to neutralize enemy advantages
Forts and the Navy
  • Built, captured, and fortified over 300 hill forts (e.g., Raigad, Sinhagad, Pratapgad, Rajgad)
  • Established a formidable navy to protect the Konkan coast from European powers (Portuguese, British) and the Siddis of Janjira
  • Kanhoji Angre, the legendary Maratha naval chief, dominated the western coast and challenged the British East India Company
  • Developed indigenous shipbuilding yards at Vijaydurg and Kolaba
Strategic Brilliance: The Maratha military system was highly adaptable. By the mid-18th century, they had also integrated European-style infantry and artillery, trained by French mercenaries, to fight conventional battles.
17th-18th Century

Administration & Economy

Efficient Governance and Revenue Systems

Administrative Framework
  • Ashtapradhan Mandal: An 8-minister council advising the king (e.g., Peshwa/PM, Amatya/Finance, Sachiv/Secretary, Senapati/Commander)
  • Provinces were divided into Prants, Sarkars, and Parganas for efficient local governance
  • Promoted Marathi as the official administrative language, alongside Sanskrit for formal documents
  • Justice was swift, with an emphasis on protecting the rights of peasants (Ryots)
Revenue & Economy
  • Chauth: A 25% tax levied on neighboring territories in exchange for protection from Maratha raids
  • Sardeshmukhi: An additional 10% tax claimed by the Chhatrapati as the hereditary head of the region
  • Land revenue was assessed based on actual crop yields, a precursor to the modern Ryotwari system
  • Thriving trade in textiles, spices, and saltpeter, with Pune emerging as a major cultural and economic hub
Economic Impact: The Maratha revenue system, while sometimes burdensome on neighboring states, revitalized the Deccan economy, encouraged agriculture, and created a prosperous class of merchants and bankers (e.g., the Punekar and Gujarati communities).
1761 CE

The Third Battle of Panipat

A Catastrophic Turning Point

The Conflict
  • Fought on January 14, 1761, between the Maratha Confederacy and the Afghan ruler Ahmad Shah Abdali
  • Abdali allied with the Nawab of Awadh and the Rohillas to halt Maratha northward expansion
  • The Maratha army, led by Sadashivrao Bhau, was massive but lacked adequate supply lines and local support
  • Trapped and starved for weeks, the Marathas were forced into a desperate, pitched battle
The Aftermath
  • The Marathas suffered a devastating defeat, with estimates of 50,000 to 70,000 soldiers killed, including Sadashivrao Bhau and many prominent chiefs
  • The defeat temporarily halted Maratha expansion into North India
  • It created a power vacuum that the British East India Company would eventually exploit
  • However, the Marathas recovered remarkably within a decade under the leadership of Mahadji Shinde
Historical Debate: While Panipat was a massive tactical defeat, modern historians argue it was not the sole cause of the empire's fall. The Maratha confederacy quickly rebuilt its military and reasserted control over Delhi by 1771.
1761-1818 CE

Decline and Fall

Internal Strife and the Anglo-Maratha Wars

Internal Fragmentation
  • After the death of the strong Peshwa Madhavrao I in 1772, the confederacy fractured
  • Fierce rivalries erupted between the Scindia, Holkar, and the Pune-based Peshwa faction
  • The system of decentralized power, once a strength, became a fatal weakness without a strong central authority
  • The British East India Company skillfully exploited these divisions through "Divide and Rule" tactics
The Anglo-Maratha Wars
  • First War (1775-1782): Ended in a stalemate with the Treaty of Salbai; Maratha prestige remained intact
  • Second War (1803-1805): British victories at Assaye and Argaon; Scindia and Bhonsle ceded vast territories
  • Third War (1817-1818): The final blow. Peshwa Baji Rao II was defeated at Khadki, Koregaon, and Mahidpur
  • In 1818, the Peshwa surrendered, the title was abolished, and the Maratha Empire was formally annexed by the British
The End of an Era: Baji Rao II was exiled to Bithur near Kanpur with a pension. The fall of the Marathas removed the last major indigenous obstacle to British supremacy in India.
LEGACY

Enduring Contributions to Indian Civilization

Swarajya Ideal

Inspired future generations, including the Indian independence movement, with the concept of self-rule

Naval Heritage

Pioneered indigenous shipbuilding and coastal defense, earning Shivaji the title "Father of the Indian Navy"

Federal Structure

The confederacy model laid early conceptual groundwork for modern Indian federalism and decentralization

Cultural Revival

Patronized Marathi literature, architecture, and Hindu temples, reversing centuries of cultural suppression
