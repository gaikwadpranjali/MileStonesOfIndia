3300-2800 BCE

Early Harappan Phase

The Formative Stage - Beginning of Agriculture and Settlements

Key Characteristics
  • Transition from nomadic hunter-gatherer lifestyle to settled agricultural communities
  • Development of early farming villages along river valleys
  • Beginning of craft specialization and pottery production
  • Initial trade networks with neighboring regions
Major Sites
  • Mehrgarh (Balochistan) - Earliest evidence of farming
  • Kot Diji (Sindh) - Fortified settlement
  • Kalibangan (Rajasthan) - Evidence of early ploughed fields
Agricultural Development
  • Cultivation of wheat, barley, peas, and sesame
  • Domestication of cattle, sheep, goats, and buffalo
  • Development of early irrigation techniques
  • Storage facilities for surplus grain
Technology: Introduction of copper tools, wheel-made pottery, and bead-making techniques. Early evidence of long-distance trade with Mesopotamia.
2800-2600 BCE

Developing Harappan Phase

Regional Integration and Urban Growth

Urban Expansion
  • Villages expanded into larger towns and proto-urban centers
  • Improved town planning with organized layouts
  • Construction of mud-brick houses and public buildings
  • Emergence of social hierarchy and administrative systems
Cultural Developments
  • Standardization of pottery styles across regions
  • Development of early seal carving with animal motifs
  • Increased production of terracotta figurines
  • Advancement in metallurgy (bronze and copper working)
Trade Networks: Expanded trade routes connecting settlements across the Indus region and with external civilizations like Mesopotamia.
2600-1900 BCE

Mature Harappan Phase

The Golden Age of Urban Civilization

Major Cities
  • Harappa - First discovered site
  • Mohenjo-Daro - "Mound of the Dead"
  • Dholavira - Water management excellence
  • Lothal - World's oldest dockyard
  • Rakhigarhi - Largest site in India
Population & Scale
  • Over 1,052 cities and settlements discovered
  • Covered 1.25 million sq km area
  • Population estimate: 1-5 million people
  • Larger than ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia combined
Achievements
  • Advanced urban planning and architecture
  • Sophisticated drainage and sanitation systems
  • Standardized weights and measures
  • Development of writing system (undeciphered)
2600 BCE

Urban Planning & Architecture

World's First Planned Cities

City Layout
  • Grid System: Precise north-south, east-west street patterns
  • Two-Tier Structure: Citadel (upper town) and Lower Town
  • Street Width: Main streets 10 meters wide, lanes 2-3 meters
  • Fortifications: Massive mud-brick walls with gates
Building Materials
  • Standardized Bricks: Ratio of 4:2:1 (length:width:thickness)
  • Kiln-fired bricks for public buildings
  • Sun-dried bricks for houses
Drainage System
  • Covered drains running along streets
  • Manholes at regular intervals for cleaning
  • Household wastewater connected to street drains
  • First civilization with such sophisticated sanitation
Notable Feature: Every house had its own well and bathroom. Wastewater was directed into covered drains, showing remarkable concern for public hygiene.
2500-2200 BCE

Trade & Commerce

Extensive Internal and International Trade Networks

International Trade
  • Mesopotamia: Major trading partner
  • Oman & Bahrain: Copper and precious stones
  • Afghanistan: Lapis lazuli and turquoise
  • Harappan seals found in Mesopotamian cities
Export Goods
  • Cotton textiles (first civilization to produce cotton)
  • Beads made from carnelian, steatite, and faience
  • Ivory products and shell ornaments
  • Timber and agricultural products
Weights & Measures
  • Standardized Weights: Binary and decimal systems
  • Weights made of chert, limestone, and steatite
  • Smallest weight: 0.05 grams
  • Measurement System: Highly accurate and uniform
  • Used across all Harappan cities
  • Facilitated fair trade practices
Lothal Dockyard: The world's oldest known dock at Lothal (Gujarat) demonstrates advanced maritime trade capabilities. The dock measured 37m x 22m.
2500 BCE

The Great Bath of Mohenjo-Daro

Remarkable Example of Ancient Engineering

Dimensions & Structure
  • Size: 12 meters long x 7 meters wide x 2.4 meters deep
  • Built with finely fitted bricks laid on edge
  • Waterproofed with gypsum and bitumen (natural tar)
  • Surrounded by colonnades and changing rooms
  • Elaborate drainage system for emptying
Purpose & Significance
  • Likely used for ritual purification ceremonies
  • Religious or ceremonial bathing
  • Evidence of organized religion and social hierarchy
  • Demonstrates advanced hydraulic engineering
Engineering Marvel: The Great Bath's waterproofing has survived for over 4,500 years, showcasing the exceptional skill of Harappan engineers.
2200-2000 BCE

Golden Age

Peak of Cultural, Economic, and Technological Achievement

Arts & Crafts
  • Seal Carving: Intricate animal motifs
  • Sculptures: Dancing Girl (bronze), Priest-King (stone)
  • Pottery: Red clay with black painted designs
  • Jewelry: Gold, silver, beads, bangles
Writing System
  • 400-700 distinct symbols identified
  • Found on seals, pottery, copper plates
  • Written right-to-left
  • Remains undeciphered to this day
Religion & Culture
  • Mother Goddess: Fertility worship
  • Pashupati: Proto-Shiva figure
  • Fire altars and ritual bathing
  • Egalitarian society (no palaces/temples)
1900 BCE

Beginning of Decline

Gradual Transformation and Urban Abandonment

Evidence of Decline
  • Abandonment of major cities (Harappa, Mohenjo-Daro)
  • Deterioration in quality of construction
  • Breakdown of urban planning standards
  • Decline in long-distance trade
  • Disappearance of writing system
Theories of Decline
  • Climate Change: Weakening monsoons, increased aridity
  • River Changes: Drying up of Saraswati/Ghaggar-Hakra river
  • Tectonic Activity: Earthquakes, flooding
  • Environmental Degradation: Deforestation, soil salinity
Important Note: Archaeological evidence shows NO signs of violent invasion or warfare. The decline was gradual, multi-causal, and resulted from environmental and economic factors.
1900-1300 BCE

Late Harappan Phase

Post-Urban Transformation and Cultural Continuity

Characteristics
  • Shift from urban to rural lifestyle
  • Smaller, scattered settlements
  • Regional cultural variations emerged
  • Simplification of pottery and crafts
  • Population migration eastward to Ganges plain
Post-Urban Cultures
  • Cemetery H Culture (Punjab) - 1900-1300 BCE
  • Jhukar Culture (Sindh) - 1900-1300 BCE
  • Rangpur Culture (Gujarat) - 1900-1200 BCE
  • Ochre Colored Pottery - Transitional phase
Legacy & Continuity
What Survived:
  • Agricultural practices and crop varieties
  • Craft traditions (pottery, bead-making)
  • Some religious symbols and rituals
What Was Lost:
  • Writing system
  • Urban sanitation systems
  • Standardized weights and measures
  • Centralized administration
UNESCO Recognition: Multiple Indus Valley sites designated as World Heritage Sites, including Mohenjo-Daro (1980) and Dholavira (2021).
