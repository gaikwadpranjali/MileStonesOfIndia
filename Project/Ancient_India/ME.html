Deep Dive: Explore the comprehensive history of India's first great empire — from Chandragupta's conquests to Ashoka's Dhamma and the dynasty's eventual fall.
322 BCE

Foundation of the Empire

Chandragupta Maurya and Chanakya's Master Plan

The Founders
  • Chandragupta Maurya: A young man of possibly humble origins (some sources claim Kshatriya lineage from the Moriya clan)
  • Chanakya (Kautilya/Vishnugupta): A brilliant Brahmin strategist and teacher at Taxila
  • Chanakya had been insulted by the Nanda king Dhana Nanda and vowed revenge
  • Together, they built a revolutionary political and military machine
The Conquest
  • Took advantage of the power vacuum left by Alexander the Great's departure (326 BCE)
  • Conquered the Nanda capital Pataliputra (modern Patna) around 322 BCE
  • Overthrew the last Nanda king and established the Mauryan dynasty
  • Unified most of the Indian subcontinent under a single rule for the first time
The Arthashastra: Chanakya's masterwork — one of the world's earliest treatises on statecraft, economics, and military strategy — became the ideological blueprint of the Mauryan state.
305 BCE

Defeat of Seleucus Nicator

Expansion to the Northwest and Diplomatic Triumph

The War
  • Seleucus Nicator, Alexander's successor, tried to reclaim eastern territories
  • Chandragupta defeated him in a major military campaign (c. 305 BCE)
  • Seleucus ceded vast territories: Gedrosia, Arachosia, Gandhara, and Paropanisadae (modern Afghanistan, Baluchistan, and parts of Pakistan)
The Marriage Alliance
  • A marriage alliance was established between the two powers
  • Seleucus sent Megasthenes as ambassador to the Mauryan court at Pataliputra
  • Megasthenes wrote the famous Indica — a detailed account of Indian society, geography, and administration
  • Chandragupta received 500 war elephants, which later helped Seleucus win the Battle of Ipsus (301 BCE)
Historical Significance: This victory established the Mauryan Empire as a major world power, equal to the Hellenistic kingdoms of the Mediterranean.
297-273 BCE

Reign of Bindusara

The Consolidator of the Empire

The "Slayer of Foes"
  • Son of Chandragupta Maurya
  • Known by titles: Amitraghata (Slayer of Foes), Amitrachates (Greek sources)
  • Ruled for approximately 25 years
  • Extended Mauryan control southward into the Deccan (all of India except Kalinga and the extreme south)
Foreign Relations
  • Maintained diplomatic ties with Hellenistic kings
  • Received ambassadors from Antiochus I and Ptolemy II
  • Requested sweet wine, dried figs, and a sophist from Antiochus — showing cultural curiosity
  • Father of 16 sons, including the future Ashoka
Jain Tradition: According to Jain texts, Chandragupta abdicated the throne, became a Jain monk, and performed Sallekhana (fasting unto death) at Shravanabelagola in Karnataka during Bindusara's reign.
268 BCE

Ashoka's Accession

The Rise of India's Greatest Emperor

The Path to the Throne
  • Initially served as governor of Ujjain and Taxila
  • After Bindusara's death, a bloody succession war lasted ~4 years
  • Ashoka reportedly killed 99 of his brothers (Buddhist sources, likely exaggerated)
  • Formally crowned in 268 BCE, though ruled effectively from 265 BCE
Titles & Legacy
  • Known as Chakravartin (universal monarch)
  • Called Devanampiya Piyadasi ("Beloved of the Gods") in his edicts
  • Ruled an empire stretching from modern Afghanistan to Bangladesh and almost all of India
  • Considered one of the greatest emperors in world history
The Transformation: Ashoka began as a ruthless conqueror (Chandashoka — "Cruel Ashoka") but transformed into Dharmashoka ("Righteous Ashoka") after the Kalinga War.
261 BCE

The Kalinga War

The Battle That Changed an Emperor — and History

The Campaign
  • Kalinga (modern Odisha) was a powerful, independent, and prosperous republic
  • Strategically vital for trade routes to South India and Southeast Asia
  • Ashoka invaded in the 8th year of his reign (261 BCE)
  • The war was fierce — Kalinga put up determined resistance
The Human Cost
  • 100,000 soldiers killed
  • 150,000 deported
  • Countless civilians died in the aftermath
  • The battlefield at Dhauli (near Bhubaneswar) was soaked in blood
Ashoka's Remorse: Walking the battlefield, Ashoka was overwhelmed by grief. He famously declared: "The conquest by Dhamma is the foremost conquest." He never waged war again.
260-232 BCE

Ashoka's Dhamma & The Edicts

A Code of Moral Governance Inscribed in Stone

Principles of Dhamma
  • Ahimsa: Non-violence toward all living beings
  • Tolerance: Respect for all religious sects
  • Compassion: Kindness to slaves, servants, and the poor
  • Obedience: Respect for parents, teachers, and elders
  • Truthfulness: Honesty in all dealings
  • Not a new religion, but a moral code for public life
The Edicts
  • Rock Edicts: 14 Major Rock Edicts carved on boulders across the empire (found at Delhi-Topra, Kalsi, Girnar, Dhauli, etc.)
  • Pillar Edicts: Inscribed on polished sandstone pillars (Lauriya Nandangarh, Allahabad, Rampurva)
  • Cave Inscriptions: Barabar Hills caves for Ajivika monks
  • Written in Prakrit using Brahmi and Kharosthi scripts
  • Some edicts in Greek and Aramaic (at Kandahar, Afghanistan)
Missionary Work: Ashoka sent his son Mahinda and daughter Sanghamitta to Sri Lanka, and missionaries to Myanmar, Thailand, Central Asia, and the Hellenistic kingdoms of Syria, Egypt, and Macedonia.
322-185 BCE

Art & Architecture

The Birth of Imperial Indian Art

Court Art
  • The 80,000 Stupas: Ashoka is said to have built thousands of stupas across the empire
  • Sanchi Stupa: Originally built by Ashoka (later expanded); now a UNESCO World Heritage Site
  • Mahabodhi Temple: Built at Bodh Gaya to mark the site of Buddha's enlightenment
  • Royal Palace at Pataliputra: Described by Megasthenes as surpassing the splendor of Persian Susa
The Pillars
  • Monolithic pillars of Chunar sandstone, up to 15 meters tall
  • Highly polished surface — called the "Mauryan Polish"
  • Crowned with animal capitals: lion, bull, elephant, horse
  • Lion Capital of Sarnath: Four lions back-to-back; now the National Emblem of India
  • Rampurva Bull: Exquisite sculpture showing Greek and Persian influences
Artistic Synthesis: Mauryan art blended indigenous Indian traditions with Persian and Hellenistic influences, creating a unique imperial style that influenced all subsequent Indian art.
322-185 BCE

Administration & Economy

A Highly Centralized Bureaucratic State

Administrative Structure
  • Centralized monarchy: The king was the supreme authority, advised by a Mantriparishad (council of ministers)
  • Provinces: Empire divided into 4-5 provinces (Atreya, Avantika, Uttarapatha, Dakshinapatha) ruled by royal princes (Kumaras)
  • Districts (Vishayas) and villages (Gramas) formed lower tiers
  • Extensive spy network (Gudhapurushas) reported directly to the king
Economy & Military
  • Agriculture: State-owned farms; land revenue (Bhaga) was 1/6th of produce
  • Trade: Flourishing internal and international trade; guilds (Shrenis) regulated crafts
  • Currency: Silver karshapanas and punch-marked coins
  • Roads: Royal highways connected Pataliputra to Taxila and the south
  • Army: 600,000 infantry, 30,000 cavalry, 9,000 war elephants (per Megasthenes)
  • War office divided into 6 boards of 5 members each
Public Works: The state built roads, rest houses, hospitals, wells, and irrigation systems. Ashoka planted medicinal herbs and banyan trees along highways for travelers.
232-185 BCE

Decline and Fall

The End of India's First Great Empire

Weak Successors
  • After Ashoka's death (232 BCE), the empire began to fragment
  • His successors were weak: Kunala, Dasharatha, Samprati, Shalishuka, Devavarman, Shatadhanvan, Brihadratha
  • The southern provinces (under the Satavahanas) broke away first
  • Revenue declined as territories shrank
Causes of Decline
  • Weak successors: No ruler of Ashoka's caliber
  • Vast empire: Difficult to administer from a single center
  • Oppression in provinces: Local governors became independent
  • Financial strain: Maintaining a huge army and bureaucracy
  • Foreign invasions: Bactrian Greeks attacked the northwest
  • Brahmanical reaction: Some scholars argue Pushyamitra opposed Ashoka's Buddhist policies
The Final Blow (185 BCE): The last Mauryan king Brihadratha was assassinated by his own general Pushyamitra Shunga during a military parade. Pushyamitra founded the Shunga dynasty, ending the Mauryan line after 137 years.
LEGACY

Enduring Contributions to World Civilization

National Symbols

The Lion Capital and Ashoka Chakra on India's flag and emblem

Non-Violence

Ashoka's Dhamma inspired Gandhi and global peace movements

Statecraft

The Arthashastra remains a masterwork of political science

Global Buddhism

Ashoka's missionaries spread Buddhism across Asia
